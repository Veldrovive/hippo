## Behavior
Pygmy hippos defecate by backing into vertical objects and whirling the tail rapidly like a propeller, flinging a urine–feces mixture over a wide area. Kranz cites Leuthold (1977) for this.
> I would say that the motion is more of a strong swinging of around 60 degrees, not a propeller. It is clear in the video

> Lang [1975] suggested that in the pygmy hippopotamus at least, this behavior is a form of territorial scent marking. The splitting of the tail hairs of these species may function to increase the hair's surface area and improve the efficiency with which the urine and feces mixture is spread in the environment. Specialized hairs for the retention, release or application of glandular secretions have been described in several mammalian species [Müller-Schwarze et al., 1977; Stoddart, 1979; Sokolov et al., 1980]; hair apparently specialized for the dispersal of other scent-marking material (urine and feces) has not been described. This hypothesis must, however, be tested further since the similar splitting of upper lip bristles in the Nile hippopotamus and the tail hairs of the white rhinoceros is not explained by it.

## Tail morphology
The tail: it is short and stout. It is nearly heart shaped, although the purpose of this exact shape is not clear from papers. Perhaps it is simply a sturdy shape. The underside of the tip carries a patch of bristle-like hairs (Fig. 2 shows them fringing the end).
The hairs:
- pale cream and about 5 cm long;
- many are split along roughly three-quarters of their length;
- each split hair divides into six or more unequal strands, splaying out like a small whisk (Fig. 3).

The anus falls below the tail attachment point so feces is produced and falls directly in to the rapidly swinging hairs.

The hypothesis is that the split hairs help break up the chunks to more effectively distribute the feces across a wider area.

## Our mechanism
A crank-rocker mechanism is built and mounted such that a 60 degree rocking motion is achieved with the center of the rocking facing straight down. The rotary motion cog is mounted to a high torque motor to drive the motion.

Just under the center of rotation is a hole through which a slurry of solids and liquids can be passed. The main tail is mounted directly to the end of the crank-rocker mechanism and falls below the slurry hole. Attached to the main tail are rigid hairs that split into smaller rigid hairs. These tairs curl slighly inward so that they fall under the slurry hole.

## Scaling
The scale of the hippo tail works well for our mechanism so scaling does not need to be accounted for.
However, we may need to account for the different momentum that our tail has in comparison to the hippo. If this ends up being the case (for example if the motor is being backdriven by the momentum of the tail switching) then we may need to scale the design smaller to reduce rotational inertia.
